MATERIALS GUIDE 2026

Paper, plastic or compostable?
Understanding the difference.

Choosing food packaging is not simply a choice between paper and plastic. Material structure, barrier, temperature, sealing and end of life all matter.

01

Start with the food, not with the material.

There is no single packaging material that is automatically the right solution for every food application.

A fresh salad, a frozen meal, a hot prepared dish and fresh protein products can require very different packaging performance.

Temperature resistance, moisture, oxygen barrier, sealing, shelf life, logistics and presentation can all influence which packaging structure is appropriate.

That is why material selection should follow the application requirements — not the other way around.

02
FIBER & PAPER

Paper-based does not always mean paper-only.

Fiber-based packaging can significantly reduce reliance on conventional fossil-based plastics, but the complete material structure matters.

Food packaging often requires resistance to grease, moisture, oxygen or heat. To achieve this performance, some paper and board packs use coatings, laminates or additional barrier layers.

These layers can change how the finished pack performs and how it should be managed at end of life.

01

Fiber content

Understand the primary material and overall pack structure.

02

Barrier

Identify how moisture, grease and gas barriers are achieved.

03

End of life

Evaluate the complete pack, not only its visible paper layer.

03
PLASTIC

Plastic can deliver high performance — but material choice still matters.

Plastics are widely used in food packaging because they can provide strong moisture barriers, sealing performance, transparency and relatively low material weight.

Different polymers, however, have different properties. PP, PET, CPET and other structures should not be treated as though they are one material.

The relevant question is not simply “Is it plastic?” but “Which polymer, in which structure, for which application and with which end-of-life route?”

04
COMPOSTABLE MATERIALS

Compostable is a technical claim, not a visual description.

A product should not be described as compostable simply because it is made from a plant-based or fiber material.

Compostability depends on the complete material structure and on the conditions under which the product is designed to break down.

Industrial compostability and home compostability are also different concepts. Certification, applicable standards and the intended end-of-life environment should therefore be understood before making a compostability claim.

IMPORTANT

“Bio-based”, “biodegradable” and “compostable” are not interchangeable terms. They describe different material characteristics and should be evaluated separately.

05

The barrier layer can change the whole packaging story.

In food packaging, the visible material is only part of the picture.

A paper tray may contain a polymer coating. A fiber pack may use a functional barrier. A plastic pack may contain multiple polymer layers.

These structures can be necessary to deliver sealing, grease resistance, moisture protection, gas barrier or shelf-life performance.

But they can also affect recyclability, compostability and the appropriate waste stream for the finished pack.

06
PERFORMANCE

Packaging has a job to do.

Sustainability cannot be evaluated independently from packaging performance.

Packaging that fails during production, leaks during transport or does not provide the required shelf life can create additional waste and operational problems.

01

Temperature

Freezing, heating, oven use and hot filling.

02

Barrier

Moisture, oxygen, grease and aroma protection.

03

Sealing

Seal integrity, machinery and production conditions.

07

Material matters. Application matters more.

Paper, plastic and compostable packaging each have different characteristics, advantages and limitations.

A meaningful comparison therefore needs to consider the complete packaging system: material structure, food protection, production requirements, logistics and end of life.
